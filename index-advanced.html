Reading contours for fish

Tight lines = drop-offs and ledges. Where contour lines bunch together the bottom falls off fast. Fish hold on the edge, moving shallow to feed and dropping deep to rest. Work the top and the base of the break.

Points. Contours that bulge out from shore in a "V" or "U" mark an underwater point. Points funnel baitfish and catch current and wind. Fish the tip and the sides where it drops into deep water.

Humps and saddles. Small closed rings out in open water are humps or underwater islands, top fish magnets. A low "saddle" between two humps or between a hump and shore is a travel route.

Channel edges. On rivers, reservoirs and bays, the old river channel shows as a deeper winding trough. Fish stack along the channel edge, especially at outside bends and where the channel swings close to a point or flat.

Inflows and outflows. Where a creek or river enters (blue NHD lines), it brings food, oxygen and cooler (or warmer) water. Outflows and dams concentrate current. Look for where the inflow's channel cuts across a flat.

Weed lines. Weeds usually stop at a set depth (often 8 to 15 ft, depending on water clarity). The contour at that depth is the weed edge. Pike, bass and perch patrol it. Satellite view can show the dark weed beds in clear water.

Flats next to deep water. A shallow flat that sits right beside a drop is a feeding table, best at dawn, dusk and on overcast days.

Near launches. Structure near ramps (🟢) and piers gets heavy pressure, but piers and docks also give shade and cover. Fishing piers (🟠/🟤) are good spots for shore anglers.

Season. Spring: shallow, warming flats and north-shore bays. Summer: deeper breaks, humps, the thermocline and shade. Fall: fish follow bait back up onto points and flats. Winter: the deepest steep breaks, slow presentations.

Salt water (Puget Sound). On NOAA charts, look for tide rips off points, ledges where soundings jump, kelp lines and the edges of channels. Fish the tide change.

Tap anywhere on the map for lat/long plus an approximate bottom elevation from the NOAA NCEI elevation model.

⚠ Not for navigation. This is a fishing-planning aid. Depths are approximate and may be outdated. Use official NOAA charts and real navigation equipment on the water. Check WDFW / state regulations before fishing.

Data sources

Coverage

• NOAA charts: US coastal waters, Puget Sound, Great Lakes, major rivers and navigable inland waters (for example, Lake Washington and the Columbia).
• WDFW lake contours: about 2,970 contour lines for selected Washington lowland lakes (zoom 12+).
• NCEI / GEBCO: worldwide coarse bathymetry. NCEI is detailed along US coasts and in some big lakes.
• Most small inland lakes outside WA have no free contour data. Paid apps such as Navionics or C-MAP are the usual source there.
